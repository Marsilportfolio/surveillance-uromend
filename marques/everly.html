En une phrase

Everly est une usine à pubs : ≈ 9 300 pubs actives sur 9 pages Facebook (3 pages à 1 500 chacune), un seul produit (huile de graines de courge en softgels, ≈ 15 $ d'appel, pack 3 bouteilles poussé), et le même récit décliné à l'infini vers 4 douleurs différentes : prostate/nuits, reins, vessie des femmes, cheveux. Uromend en a 133.

Mise à jour du 23/10/2026 : les pages « docteur » ont disparu

  • 5 pages sur 9 sont tombées à 0 pub en deux jours : Dr. Lisa Downing (1 500 le 21/10), Nutritionist Katherine Davis (1 500), Dr. Anastasia Chamberlen (1 600), Elemental Grove (950), NaturaNoura (270). Même en cherchant les pubs inactives, la Bibliothèque n'affiche plus rien pour Dr. Lisa Downing : l'historique a été effacé, ce qui arrive en général quand Meta supprime ou restreint une page (ici, des faux « docteurs » et « nutritionnistes »).
  • Total Everly : 9 280 → 3 600 pubs (-61 %). Restent Everly (1 400), Rooted Remedies (1 000), Nutragrace (950), Elizabeth Miller (250).
  • Ce qui tourne encore est surtout une pub catalogue « Introducing Everly Pumpkin Seed Oil » (cheveux + levers la nuit + bouffées de chaleur) qui envoie directement sur la fiche produit shopeverly.shop, déclinée des centaines de fois : plus d'advertorial (faux article) signé par un « docteur ».
  • Dans les classements de recherche, Everly passe de 25 pubs vues à 1 (« saw palmetto » : 2e → 22e). Sur « Flomax », c'est maintenant Elivora qui tient la 1re place.
  • Leçon pour Uromend : les personnages « Dr. » / « Nutritionist, PhD » inventés font gagner vite, mais Meta peut tout couper d'un coup. Nos témoignages doivent rester de vraies personnes ou des personnages clairement fictifs, sans faux titre médical.

Angles marketing (ce qu'on lit dans 84 pubs récoltées)

  • La peur de la sonde / de la chirurgie : « My husband wore a catheter bag strapped to his thigh for 19 days », « My husband had prostate surgery 8 weeks ago ». La sonde est l'image d'horreur récurrente, toujours vue par la femme.

Cible

  • Hommes de plus de 45-50 ans qui se lèvent 3 à 6 fois par nuit, déjà sous Flomax/tamsulosine/finastéride, qui ont peur de la sonde et de l'opération.
  • Leurs femmes : énormément de pubs sont écrites à la première personne par l'épouse ou une infirmière (« My husband… »). La femme est celle qui achète pour lui.
  • Hommes et femmes de plus de 40 ans inquiets pour leurs reins (eGFR, dialyse, urine mousseuse).
  • Femmes de plus de 45 ans : fuites, serviettes, cheveux qui tombent, thyroïde.

Promesses

  • Dormir toute la nuit sans se lever ; jet fort ; vider la vessie ; « block the DHT ».
  • Éviter la sonde, la chirurgie, la dialyse (promesse par la peur).
  • Résultats en « 8 weeks » / « 12 weeks » ; « wake up with real energy ».
  • Cheveux plus épais, moins de fuites (femmes).

But I know what three points means. Three points, twice a year, for five years — that's the difference between living your life and sitting in a chair with a tube in your arm three days a week.
The one I couldn't let go of was Arthur.
He was 63. Retired shop teacher. Hands like leather. He used to bring homemade birdhouses to the clinic for the nurses. Whittled them himself. He came in every six months like clockwork. GFR 48. Then 44. Then 39. Then 31.

That was the one that broke something in me.

By year two? He was still getting up three to four times a night. The tamsulosin had taken him from five times down to three — and then stopped working. His prostate was still growing. The drug was relaxing the muscle but doing nothing about the growth. He was exhausted. Dark circles under his eyes that never went away. The kind of tired that coffee doesn't touch because it's not about energy — it's about never getting four unbroken hours of sleep.
By year four? The stream was barely a stream. He'd stand at the urinal at a restaurant and wait. And wait. Thirty seconds before anything started. Then a weak, hesitant trickle that stopped and started while the guy next to him finished and walked away. He started using stalls instead. Then he stopped going to restaurants.
And the urgency. God, the urgency. The sudden, overwhelming need to go — immediately — that hit in the car, at the store, in the middle of his grandson's baseball game. He mapped every bathroom within a ten-minute radius of anywhere he went. His world got smaller every month. Road trips disappeared. Movies disappeared. Sitting through a full church service became an exercise in clock-watching and anxiety.
He wasn't sick. He was watching his prostate squeeze the life out of his daily routine while his doctor adjusted the dosage on a drug that had already stopped working.
And every single doctor told him the same thing.

"Tamsulosin will relax the smooth muscle and improve your flow."
"Finasteride can shrink the prostate over time. Six to twelve months to see results."
"Lifestyle modifications — reduce caffeine, limit fluids after 6 PM."
"If the medication stops working, we can discuss surgical options. TURP is the gold standard."
One of them — and this still makes me clench my jaw — told him that BPH is just a normal part of aging and he should accept that his bathroom habits would be "different going forward." He was 60 years old. He'd just retired. He was supposed to be traveling with my mom, fishing with his buddies, enjoying the years he'd worked his entire life to reach. And a urologist was telling him to accept that he'd spend those years mapping bathrooms and sleeping in ninety-minute intervals.
On medication. Getting worse. Every year.
